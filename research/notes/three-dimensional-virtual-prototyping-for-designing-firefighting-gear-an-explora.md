## Abstract
Guided by the Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) framework, this study explores how firefighting gear manufacturers perceive 3D apparel visualization software and how these perceptions shape their intentions to adopt the technology. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 12 experts from six companies producing wildland and structural turnout gear, including pattern makers, designers, technical designers, and a business manager. Content analysis revealed that manufacturers viewed 3D apparel visualization software as a useful tool for pattern optimization, visualizing air gaps, and improving communication with municipal customers. However, participants also highlighted significant technical limitations, particularly in simulating composite materials, multilayer garment structures, and substantial ease and air gap behavior in structural turnout gear. They also noted constraints related to organizational slack, including financial and human resource demands and disruptions to established workflows. Adoption decisions were further shaped by key industry characteristics, such as stringent National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) standards, unchanging garment style requirements, conservative industry norms, and varying levels of business partner readiness. The findings extend TOE research to a specialized functional clothing context by highlighting sector-specific constraints and the importance of distinguishing between current capabilities and perceived future value. Practical implications are offered for software developers, regulators, and manufacturers seeking to align digital design investments with the technological and regulatory realities of firefighting gear innovation.

This gap is notable given the technology's potential to enhance the wearability, performance, and cost-effectiveness of such gear (Park et al., [2014a](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR56 "Park, H., Kim, S., Wu, Y., & Allen, N. \(2014a\).                   https://doi.org/10.1186/s40691-014-0008-3

In current manufacturing practice, firefighting gear design relies heavily on iterative physical sampling, manual pattern adjustments, and text-based communication with contract factories and municipal customers (Jung et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR26 "Jung, U., Porterfield, A., & Kwon, C. \(2022\).                   https://doi.org/10.31274/itaa.16001
                  
                ")). These steps can be time- and resource-intensive and may limit the ability to evaluate key PPE characteristics, such as air gap volume and size-dependent range of motion, in relation to NFPA regulations. Three-dimensional apparel visualization directly addresses these limitations by enabling virtual prototyping prior to physical sampling.
Addressing this gap, the present study aims to explore how firefighting gear manufacturers perceive 3D apparel visualization software and how evaluations of specific software attributes shape organizational openness to adoption. By centering a safety–critical functional apparel context, the study highlights the potential of 3D apparel visualization software to function as a shared visual language among manufacturers and user groups, improving communication around design features and reducing the costs associated with iterative development. Guided by Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990)’s Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) framework, this study further investigates how technological, organizational, and environmental conditions influence firms’ intentions to adopt 3D apparel visualization software. In doing so, it extends technology adoption research by applying the framework to specialized protective clothing and by foregrounding organizational-level perspectives that remain underrepresented in prior 3D apparel visualization software research.
The findings provide practical insights for firefighting gear manufacturers considering 3D apparel visualization software adoption and inform software developers and technology providers about industry-specific needs, perceived benefits, and adoption-related concerns relevant to functional and protective apparel design. This study is part of a larger action research project investigating the integration of 3D apparel visualization software into the workflow of a wildland firefighting gear manufacturer. While the broader project addresses systemic and operational implementation challenges, the present paper focuses specifically on manufacturers’ perceptions, offering a distinct contribution to understanding digital transformation in this specialized protective clothing sector.
## Literature Review
### Emerging 3D apparel visualization software
Emerging 3D apparel visualization software (e.g., CLO3D, Optitex, and Browzwear) links 2D pattern engineering with physics-based simulation to generate virtual prototypes that can be iterated before physical sampling. Across platforms, teams typically import or draft patterns, virtually stitch and drape garments on parametric avatars, and iteratively adjust pattern geometry while monitoring fit-relevant diagnostics (e.g., tension/strain maps) to assess ease distribution, balance, and stress concentration during technical review and approvals (Baria et al., [2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR73 "Baria, B., Shahid, M.                   https://doi.org/10.1177/00405175251360399
                  
                "); Choi, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR10 "Choi, K. H. \(2022\). 3D dynamic fashion design development using digital technology and its potential in online platforms. Fashion and Textiles, 9, Article 9. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1186/s40691-021-00286-1
                  
                "); Lee & Park, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR31 "Lee, E., & Park, H. \(2017\). 3D virtual fit simulation technology: Strengths and areas of improvement for increased industry adoption. International Journal of Fashion Design, Technology and Education, 10\(1\), 59–70. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/17543266.2016.1194483
                  
                ")). Because updates to pattern pieces, colorways, materials, and finishing details can be simulated and rendered immediately, these systems accelerate design iteration and strengthen communication between design and technical teams (Choi, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR10 "Choi, K.                   https://doi.org/10.1177/00405175251360399

                  https://doi.org/10.1177/15589250231194621
                  
                ")). Prior industry cases also illustrate how 3D assets may be repurposed across merchandising, wholesale, and digital channels, including e-commerce and AR-oriented applications (ByondXR, [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR7 "ByondXR. \(2019, December 4\). Looking ahead, scholars anticipate continued expansion into consumer-facing experiences such as digital fitting rooms that can support customization, reduce returns, and enable sell-and-then-make models (Nissen & Nerup, [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR50 "Nissen, B. More broadly, the interpretability and decision value of diagnostic outputs can vary by garment types, body region, and movement condition (Brubacher et al., [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR6 "Brubacher, K., Tyler, D., Apeagyei, P., Venkatraman, P., & Brownridge, A. M. \(2023\). Evaluation of the accuracy and practicability of predicting compression garment pressure using virtual fit technology. Clothing and Textiles Research Journal, 41\(2\), 107–124. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X21999314
                  
                "); Hakeem et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR19 "Hakeem, F., Xia, S., & Stannard, C. \(2024\). Evaluating 3D apparel simulation technology’s performance of creative pattern-cutting techniques with the assistance of ASVT. Advance online publication. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X241278929
                  
                ")). At the organizational level, returns may be modest when adoption is pursued without complementary process redesign and supporting data infrastructure, including robust fabric libraries and compatible PLM structures (Magnus et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR83 "Magnus, K.-H., Ibáñez, P., Scalco, A., & Hügl, J. \(2024, May 24\). Reimagining the apparel value chain amid volatility. McKinsey & Company. 
                  https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/retail/our-insights/reimagining-the-apparel-value-chain-amid-volatility
                  
                ")). Usability challenges persist as well, particularly around avatar realism and occasional uncanny valley reactions that can constrain consumer-facing applications (Choi, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR10 "Choi, K. H. \(2022\). 3D dynamic fashion design development using digital technology and its potential in online platforms. Fashion and Textiles, 9, Article 9. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1186/s40691-021-00286-1
                  
                ")). Taken together, these limitations underscore the need for this study by highlighting the importance of exploring the factors that influence the adoption and scalability of 3D apparel visualization software at the firm level.
### 3D apparel visualization software for firefighting gear
Although commercial 3D apparel visualization software is widely used in fashion and sportswear, their application to safety–critical protective clothing introduces additional requirements because design and fit must support standards-based safety performance. In firefighting PPE, these requirements include thermal protection (e.g., thermal resistance, insulation, TPP/THL), adequate coverage at garment and interface areas, mobility while carrying operational loads such as self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA), and compliance with standardized test criteria (Jin et al., [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR24 "Jin, L., Cao, M. L., Yu, W., Hu, J. Y., Yoon, K. J., Park, P. K., & Li, Y. \(2018\). New approaches to evaluate the performance of firefighter protective clothing materials. Fire Technology, 54, 1283–1307. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s10694-018-0730-2
                  
                "); National Fire Protection Association, [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR46 "National Fire Protection Association. \(2018\). NFPA 1971: Standard on protective ensembles for structural fire fighting and proximity fire fighting. 
                  https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-1971-standard-development/1971
                  
                "), [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR47 "National Fire Protection Association. \(2022\). NFPA 1977: Standard on protective clothing and equipment for wildland fire fighting and urban interface fire fighting. 
                  https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-1977-standard-development/1977
                  
                "); Sokolowski et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR87 "Sokolowski, S. L., Griffi n, L., Wu, Y., McKinney, E., Morris, K., & Bettencourt, C. \(2022\). Examination of current US female fi refi ghting personal protective equipment \(PPE\) sizing and fitting process challenges: an opportunity to improve safety. Fashion and Textiles, 9\(1\), 40. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1186/s40691-022-00314-8
                  
                ")). These constraints vary by gear type. Wildland and urban-interface protective clothing is typically single-layer and uniform-like (Stull & Stull, [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR67 "Stull, J. O., & Stull, G. \(2021\). Contamination and cleaning of wildland firefighting gear. FireRescue1. 
                  https://www.firerescue1.com/fire-products/personal-protective-equipment-ppe/articles/contamination-and-cleaning-of-wildland-firefighting-gear-DolBEvMyz1B7rObW/
                  
                ")) but remains subject to specialized design, sizing, and testing requirements (National Fire Protection Association, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR47 "National Fire Protection Association. \(2022\). NFPA 1977: Standard on protective clothing and equipment for wildland fire fighting and urban interface fire fighting. 
                  https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-1977-standard-development/1977
                  
                ")). Structural firefighting ensembles, by contrast, are multilayer systems composed of an outer shell, moisture barrier, and thermal liner, with performance evaluated against standards specifying minimum protection from thermal and related hazards (McQuerry et al., [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR41 "McQuerry, M., DenHartog, E., & Barker, R. \(2017\). Evaluating turnout composite layering strategies for reducing thermal burden in structural firefighter protective clothing systems. Textile Research Journal, 87\(10\), 1217–1225. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0040517516651101
                  
                "); National Fire Protection Association, [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR46 "National Fire Protection Association. \(2018\). NFPA 1971: Standard on protective ensembles for structural fire fighting and proximity fire fighting. 
                  https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-1971-standard-development/1971
                  
                ")). System level test methods, including manikin-based thermal exposure protocols such as NFPA 2112 and ASTM F1930, underscore that thermal protection performance is evaluated at the full-ensemble system level, rather than as an appearance- or comfort-driven outcome (ASTM International, 2018; National Fire Protection Association, [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR48 "National Fire Protection Association. \(2023\). NFPA 2112: Standard on flame-resistant clothing for protection of industrial personnel against short-duration thermal exposures from fire. 
                  https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-2112-standard-development/2112
                  
                ")).
Within structural firefighting ensembles, the air gap between the body and clothing layers is a safety-relevant design factor because it directly influences heat transfer, mobility, and comfort. Prior studies have quantified air gap size and distribution using 3D body scanning to characterize protective garment fit (Lu et al., [2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR35 "Lu, Y., Song, G., & Li, J. \(2014\). A novel approach for fit analysis of thermal protective clothing using three-dimensional body scanning. Applied Ergonomics, 45\(6\), 1439–1446. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2014.04.007
                  
                ")) and have linked air gap characteristics to thermal performance outcomes (Lu et al., [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR36 "Lu, Y., Song, G., Li, J., & Wang, F. \(2015\). The impact of air gap on thermal performance of protective clothing against hot water spray. Textile Research Journal, 85\(7\), 709–721. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0040517514553875
                  
                ")). Given the posture- and movement-intensive nature of firefighting work, dynamic approaches to garment performance testing are particularly important. Research has demonstrated that air gap thickness and distribution change during motion (Hu et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR22 "Hu, P., Ho, E. S. L., Aslam, N., Komura, T., & Shum, H. P. H. \(2019\). A new method to evaluate the dynamic air gap thickness and garment sliding of virtual clothes during walking. Textile Research Journal, 89\(19–20\), 4148–4161. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0040517519826930
                  
                ")) and vary systematically across work-relevant postures and tasks (Psikuta et al., [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR61 "Psikuta, A., Sherif, F., Mert, E., Mandal, S., & Annaheim, S. \(2023\). Clothing air gaps in various postures in firefighters’ work. International Journal of Biometeorology, 67\(1\), 121–131. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s00484-022-02391-2
                  
                ")). Collectively, this body of work highlights heterogeneous, posture-dependent air gaps as central to thermal protection, yet still insufficiently represented in predictive modeling and experimental frameworks.
In this context, 3D apparel visualization software can be useful when it can represent multilayer assemblies, movement constraints, and garment-to-body space in ways that support standards-compliant decision making. However, much of the existing 3D apparel visualization software literature remains focused on appearance-driven apparel applications rather than on the standardized performance criteria and manufacturing constraints that define firefighting PPE. This gap underscores the need for manufacturer-centered evidence on how 3D tools are adopted, interpreted, and constrained within real-world PPE development pipelines.
### Applications of 3D apparel visualization software in designing functional garments
Scholarly work has increasingly conceptualized 3D apparel visualization software as a method for evaluation and an engineering resource in functional garment design, particularly in contexts where conventional sizing systems and static fit assumptions fall short. A central application is the use of 3D body data (e.g., scans or scan-derived avatars) to represent nonstandard morphology and posture-dependent body configurations so that pattern modifications reflect wear-context requirements rather than a single standing reference body. This includes scoliosis-informed workflows that link garment modeling to skeletal and scan data (Mosleh et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR44 "Mosleh, S., Abtew, M. A., Bruniaux, P., Tartare, G., & Xu, Y. \(2021\). Developments of adapted clothing for physically disabled people with scoliosis using 3D geometrical model. Applied Sciences, 11\(22\), Article 10655. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/app112210655
                  
                ")), maternity scan-to-simulation approaches across gestational stages (Cieśla et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR11 "Cieśla, K., Frydrych, I., Krzywinski, S., & Kyosev, Y. \(2020\). Design workflow for virtual design of clothing for pregnant women. Communications in Development and Assembling of Textile Products, 1\(2\), 148–159. 
                  https://doi.org/10.25367/cdatp.2020.1.p148-159
                  
                ")), and age-related morphology where morphed 3D models are translated into ergonomic blocks and adapted into compression prototypes (Lee & Park, [2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR32 "Lee, S. H., & Park, J. \(2025\). Ergonomic block patterns for older women’s athleisure pants: Applying the 3D surface flattening and pattern reduction method. Advance online publication. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X251351356
                  
                ")). In protective PPE contexts, posture-specific scanning is used to capture task-relevant dimensional shifts, underscoring that the “fit body” may be activity-specific rather than static (Rudolf et al., [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR62 "Rudolf, A., Bogović, S., Rogina-Car, B., Stjepanovič, Z., Jevšnik, S., & Cupar, A. \(2017\). Virtual prototyping of special protective clothing for sport aircraft pilots. In Book of abstracts: 12th Joint International Conference CLOTECH 2017 on innovative materials & technologies in made-up textile articles, protective clothing and footwear \(pp. 84–96\). Lodz University of Technology.")).
Within these functional and protective applications, diagnostic outputs in 3D apparel visualization software (e.g., strain, pressure, and distance indicators) are typically interpreted as decision supports for locating constraint zones and prioritizing pattern revisions under movement demands. Rudolf et al. ([2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR62 "Rudolf, A., Bogović, S., Rogina-Car, B., Stjepanovič, Z., Jevšnik, S., & Cupar, A. \(2017\). Virtual prototyping of special protective clothing for sport aircraft pilots. In Book of abstracts: 12th Joint International Conference CLOTECH 2017 on innovative materials & technologies in made-up textile articles, protective clothing and footwear \(pp. 84–96\). Lodz University of Technology.")), for example, used tension visualizations to identify high-tension regions in protective overalls and to guide reconstruction of seated-posture garment patterns suited for cockpit movement. Related studies similarly employ map-based diagnostics to justify targeted modifications for adaptive and functional garments (Rudolf, [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR63 "Rudolf, A., Cupar, A., & Stjepanovič, Z. \(2019\). Designing functional garments for people with physical disabilities or kyphosis using computer simulation techniques. Industria Textila, 70\(2\), 182–191. 
                  https://doi.org/10.35530/IT.070.02.1592
                  
                "); Mosleh et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR44 "Mosleh, S., Abtew, M. A., Bruniaux, P., Tartare, G., & Xu, Y. \(2021\). Developments of adapted clothing for physically disabled people with scoliosis using 3D geometrical model. Applied Sciences, 11\(22\), Article 10655. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/app112210655
                  
                ")). Importantly, functional studies often emphasize calibration and interpretation rather than visualization alone. Lee and Park ([2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR32 "Lee, S. H., & Park, J. \(2025\). Ergonomic block patterns for older women’s athleisure pants: Applying the 3D surface flattening and pattern reduction method. Advance online publication. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X251351356
                  
                ")), for instance, combined software-based objective clothing-pressure measures with wearer ratings of fit, appearance, and mobility to define acceptable tradeoffs between supportive compression and discomfort, and sport/performance studies similarly leverage posture-specific analyses.
Recent research also positions 3D apparel visualization software as an infrastructure for collaboration and user-centered decision-making in functional contexts. Hudson and Hwang ([2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR80 "Hudson, K., & Hwang, C. \(2022\). Application of 3D prototyping to promote size-inclusive design practices for plus-size apparel. Fashion Practice, 14\(1\), 5–25. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/17569370.2020.1804152
                  
                ")), drawing on the FEA framework, show that virtual prototyping can integrate functional requirements and aesthetic preferences while supporting communication among designers, evaluators, and end users. Lee and Park ([2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR32 "Lee, S. H., & Park, J. \(2025\). Ergonomic block patterns for older women’s athleisure pants: Applying the 3D surface flattening and pattern reduction method. Advance online publication. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0887302X251351356
                  
                ")) likewise used wearer feedback to interpret preferences across reduction-rate prototypes, helping define acceptable design tradeoffs. Porterfield and Lamar ([2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR60 "Porterfield, A., & Lamar, T. A. \(2021\). A framework for incorporating virtual fitting into the costume design and production process. International Journal of Fashion Design, Technology and Education, 14\(1\), 91–100. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/17543266.2020.1864484
                  
                ")) extend the communicative value of virtual fitting to costume development, where early visualization facilitated decisions and reduced reliance on limited in-person fitting time.
Nevertheless, it remains underexplored how these demonstrated capabilities translate to protective clothing environments constrained by regulatory standards, certification, material systems, and scalable manufacturability. Empirical evidence is still limited on how 3D apparel visualization software workflows are integrated into manufacturers’ production systems for commercially produced PPE.
### Firms’ perceptions of 3D apparel visualization software: the technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework
According to Schmitt et al. ([2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")), the TOE framework helps identify characteristics of innovative concepts that influence their implementation at the firm level. Originally proposed by Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990), the TOE framework conceptualizes a firm’s intention to adopt an innovation as being shaped by three contextual factors: technological, organizational, and environmental, which together present both opportunities and constraints for adoption (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). This framework has been widely used to explain and predict technology adoption and is considered one of the most dominant and contextually specific models for studying enterprise-level innovation adoption (Awa et al., 2017; Gangwar et al., [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR16 "Gangwar, H., Date, H., & Ramaswamy, R. \(2015\). Understanding determinants of cloud computing adoption using an integrated TAM–TOE model. Journal of Enterprise Information Management, 28\(1\), 107–130. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1108/JEIM-08-2013-0065
                  
                ")). Prior research also highlights that the framework provides a flexible analytical structure, as determinants within each context can be tailored to the specific technology under examination (Oliveira & Martins, [2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR51 "Oliveira, T., & Martins, M. F. \(2011\). Literature review of information technology adoption models at firm level. Electronic Journal of Information Systems Evaluation, 14\(1\), 110–121.")). Building on this perspective, researchers extending the TOE framework argue that employees are more likely to adopt firm-level innovations when they (a) perceive clear performance benefits or value relative to costs and existing practices and (b) feel technically capable of using the innovation effectively (Aligarh, 2023; Gupta et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR79 "Gupta, S., Ghardallou, W., Pandey, D. K., & Sahu, G. P. \(2022\). Artifi cial intelligence adoption in the insurance industry: Evidence using the technology–organization–environment framework. Research in International Business and Finance, 63, 101757. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ribaf.2022.101757
                  
                "); Kuan & Chau, [2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR30 "Kuan, K. K. Y., & Chau, P. Y. K. \(2001\). A perception-based model for EDI adoption in small businesses using a technology–organization–environment framework. Information & Management, 38\(8\), 507–521. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/S0378-7206\(01\)00073-8
                  
                ")).
#### Technological context
The technological context refers to both internal and external variables relevant to firms and industries, including legacy systems and current technological practices (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). In the context of technology adoption, this dimension closely aligns with perceived behavioral control, reflecting users' agility and adoption decisions shaped by their skills as well as the support resources necessary to fully leverage new systems (Awa et al., [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR2 "Awa, H. O., Ukoha, O., & Emecheta, B. C. \(2016\). Using TOE theoretical framework to study the adoption of ERP solution. Cogent Business & Management, 3\(1\), Article 1196571. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/23311975.2016.1196571
                  
                ")). A firm's technical expertise, software proficiency, IT infrastructure, and compatibility with existing hardware and software are critical factors influencing adoption (Jin et al., [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR23 "Jin, D., Chai, K.-H., & Tan, K.-C. \(2012\). Organizational adoption of new service development tools. Managing Service Quality: An International Journal, 22\(3\), 233–259. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1108/09604521211230978
                  
                "); Yan & Fiorito, [2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR71 "Yan, H., & Fiorito, S. S. \(2007\). CAD/CAM diffusion and infusion in the U.S. apparel industry. Journal of Fashion Marketing and Management: An International Journal, 11\(2\), 238–245. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1108/13612020710751400
                  
                ")). For 3D apparel visualization software specifically, technological challenges such as establishing virtual trim and fabric libraries, resolving incompatibilities among different 3D tools, and addressing interoperability and file format limitations can hinder seamless integration with existing systems (Papahristou & Bilalis, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR54 "Papahristou, E., & Bilalis, N. \(2017\). 3D virtual prototyping traces new avenues for fashion design and product development: A qualitative study. Journal of Textile Science & Engineering, 7\(2\), Article 297. 
                  https://doi.org/10.4172/2165-8064.1000297
                  
                ")). Additionally, technology maturity, the degree to which a technological architecture is ready to be deployed within a firm, is particularly important for early adopters of emerging technologies (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")).
#### Organizational context
The organizational context refers to the inherent characteristics and resources of the firm (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). Research indicates that technology adoption frequently encounters resistance when decision-makers and end-users hold divergent views, resulting in implementation challenges (Park & DeLong, [2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR58 "Park, J., & DeLong, M. \(2009\). Understanding new technology adoption in the apparel and footwear industry within a social framework: A case of rapid prototyping technology. International Journal of Fashion Design, Technology and Education, 2\(2–3\), 101–112. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/17543260903349007
                  
                ")). Top management's understanding of a technology's benefits and its commitment to fostering a supportive climate, culture, vision, and set of values are among the most influential determinants of adoption (Awa et al., [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR2 "Awa, H. O., Ukoha, O., & Emecheta, B. C. \(2016\). Using TOE theoretical framework to study the adoption of ERP solution. Cogent Business & Management, 3\(1\), Article 1196571. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/23311975.2016.1196571
                  
                "); Balaid et al., [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR3 "Balaid, A., Abd Rozan, M., Hikmi, S. N., & Memon, J. \(2016\). Knowledge maps: A systematic literature review and directions for future research. International Journal of Information Management, 36\(3\), 451–475. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2016.02.005
                  
                ")). Firm size is another critical organizational factor. Larger enterprises, supported by greater resources and infrastructure, tend to be more inclined to adopt new technologies (Henao-Ramírez & López-Zapata, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR88 "Henao-Ramírez, A. M., & Lopez-Zapata, E. \(2022\). Analysis of the factors infl uencing adoption of 3D design digital technologies in Colombian fi rms. Journal of Enterprise Information Management, 35\(2\), 429–454. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1108/JEIM-10-2020-0416
                  
                ")). They possess higher levels of financial readiness and benefit from economies of scale, allowing them to absorb the risks or potential failures associated with resource-intensive innovation-oriented initiatives involving network externalities (Levenburg et al., [2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR33 "Levenburg, N. M., Magal, S. R., & Kosalge, P. \(2006\). An exploratory investigation of organizational factors and e-business motivations among SMEs in the U.S. Electronic Markets, 16\(1\), 70–84. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/10196780500491070
                  
                ")). In contrast, without adequate financial capacity, firms may lack both the infrastructure and the technical professionals required to implement and sustain new systems (Maduku et al., [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR37 "Maduku, D. K., Mpinganjira, M., & Duh, H. \(2016\). Understanding mobile marketing adoption intention by South African SMEs: A multi-perspective framework. International Journal of Information Management, 36\(5\), 711–723. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2016.04.018
                  
                ")).
#### Environmental context
The environmental context refers to the external setting in which a firm operates (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990) note that this context encompasses competitors, industry conditions, governmental regulations, and broader societal factors. Competitive pressure is a central driver of adoption. When competitors implement new technologies, firms often feel compelled to do the same in order to maintain industry standards and performance expectations (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). Studies show that firms facing stronger market competition are more likely to adopt 3D design technologies (Henao-Ramírez & López-Zapata, [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR88 "Henao-Ramírez, A. M., & Lopez-Zapata, E. \(2022\). Analysis of the factors infl uencing adoption of 3D design digital technologies in Colombian fi rms. Journal of Enterprise Information Management, 35\(2\), 429–454. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1108/JEIM-10-2020-0416
                  
                ")). Regulatory factors also shape adoption decisions. Regulatory complexity and policy uncertainty can constrain adoption, whereas clear governmental policies, rules, and legal frameworks help prevent misuse and support technological implementation (Gupta et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR79 "Gupta, S., Ghardallou, W., Pandey, D. K., & Sahu, G. P. \(2022\). Artifi cial intelligence adoption in the insurance industry: Evidence using the technology–organization–environment framework. Research in International Business and Finance, 63, 101757. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ribaf.2022.101757
                  
                ")). Moreover, systematically managing relationships with stakeholders can enhance organizational performance (Guoyou et al., [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR18 "Guoyou, Q., Saixing, Z., Chiming, T., Haitao, Y., & Hailiang, Z. \(2013\). Stakeholders’ influences on corporate green innovation strategy: A case study of manufacturing firms in China. Corporate Social Responsibility and Environmental Management, 20\(1\), 1–14. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1002/csr.283
                  
                ")). In the global apparel supply chain, international partnerships further influence perceptions of 3D apparel visualization software. For example, South Korean sourcing agents and manufacturers working with U.S. apparel firms view 3D apparel visualization software as beneficial for reducing lead times and improving communication, whereas firms serving primarily domestic markets express lower enthusiasm for adoption (Hwang Shin & Lee, [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR86 "Hwang Shin, S. J., & Lee, H. \(2020\). The use of 3D virtual fi tting technology: Comparison between sourcing agents, contractors, and domestic suppliers in the apparel industry. International Journal of Fashion Design, Technology and Education, 13\(3\), 300–307. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/17543266.2020.1797905
                  
                ")). Additionally, consumer perceptions of innovative technologies also affect firms' decisions to implement them (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")).
Taken together, these external pressures and stakeholder expectations help explain why adoption of 3D apparel visualization software remains uneven across firms, even as the technology becomes more visible in the broader apparel sector. Accordingly, the TOE framework is well suited for this study because it enables us to explore how environmental forces interact with firms’ technological capabilities and organizational readiness to shape adoption decisions and implementation barriers in manufacturing contexts.
## Methods
This study explores firefighting gear manufacturers’ perceptions of adopting 3D apparel visualization software, addressing a gap in the literature on functional clothing development. Given the emerging nature of this topic, a qualitative approach was employed due to its suitability for investigating underexplored phenomena (Barbour & Barbour, [2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR4 "Barbour, R. S., & Barbour, M. \(2003\). Evaluating and synthesizing qualitative research: The need to develop a distinctive approach. Journal of Evaluation in Clinical Practice, 9\(2\), 179–186. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2753.2003.00371.x
                  
                ")). To generate in-depth insights, semi-structured interviews were conducted with manufacturers of firefighting personal protective clothing and equipment (Rutledge & Hogg, [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR64 "Rutledge, P. B., & Hogg, J. L. C. \(2020\). In-depth interviews. In K. E. Dill-Shackleford, D. R. Riva, & J. E. N. Van der Linden \(Eds.\), The international encyclopedia of media psychology. Wiley.")). This approach enabled a nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences, attitudes, and perceived challenges related to the use of 3D apparel visualization software. Data were collected from multiple companies to enhance the rigor of the study by incorporating diverse perspectives and organizational contexts (Denzin, [2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR13 "Denzin, N. The findings provide valuable insights into the factors shaping software adoption and offer practical implications for industry stakeholders regarding the feasibility and potential benefits of integrating 3D apparel visualization software into firefighting gear design and production. ### Data collection
This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the approved protocols. Professionals working in product development and technical design roles were purposefully recruited to participate in in-depth interviews conducted via Zoom in March and April 2023. A total of twelve representatives from six firefighting gear manufacturing companies took part in approximately two-hour interviews, with one to three participants representing each company.
The semi-structured interview protocol included targeted probes to capture participants’ prior experience with 3D apparel visualization software and its application in their work. The questions were designed to examine manufacturers’ perceptions of the software’s usefulness in firefighting gear design, as well as the challenges they had encountered or anticipated. The interview guide was adapted from Cobos et al. ([2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR12 "Cobos, L. M., Mejia, C., Ozturk, A. B., & Wang, Y. \(2016\). A technology adoption and implementation process in an independent hotel chain. International Journal of Hospitality Management, 57, 93–105. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2016.06.005
                  
                ")) and Foster (2017) and modified to reflect the firefighting gear context. To gauge broader industry awareness, participants were also asked about the perceived prevalence of 3D apparel visualization software within the sector. Example questions included: “What factors should be considered when adopting 3D apparel visualization software in firefighting gear manufacturing?”, “Based on your current understanding, how might this technology meet the needs of you and your company?”, and “What do you perceive as the primary drivers and barriers to implementing 3D apparel visualization software in the firefighting gear industry?”.
A separate pilot interview was not conducted. Instead, the interview guide was refined through iterative review and discussion among the research team to ensure clarity and alignment with the study’s conceptual framework and industry context. For participants unfamiliar with 3D apparel visualization software, the primary investigator provided a brief overview using the CLO 3D and Optitex 3D websites to demonstrate the capabilities and potential applications of these tools.
### Participant profiles
Twelve experts actively involved in firefighting gear design and development participated in this study, representing six U.S.-based manufacturing companies (Table [1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#Tab1)). Across these companies (labeled A-F), participants held roles including designer, technical designer, pattern maker, product innovation manager, and business manager.
The companies varied in both product specialization and organizational size. Two companies (A and D) primarily produced wildland fire gear, three (B, C, and F) focused on structural turnout gear, and one (E) manufactured both types. Organizational scale ranged from very small firms with fewer than 25 employees (Company A) and small firms with fewer than 100 employees (Company E), to medium-sized companies with fewer than 1,000 employees (Companies B and C), a larger company with fewer than 1,500 employees (Company D), and a very large multinational firm with more than 10,000 employees (Company F). This variation captures perspectives from both small, specialized manufacturers and large-scale enterprises within the firefighting gear industry.
Among the six companies, two (B and D) had already implemented 3D apparel visualization software in their design and development processes, whereas the remaining four (A, C, E, and F) had not yet adopted such tools. Collectively, these participants offer a diverse range of manufacturer perspectives on the factors that facilitate or hinder the adoption of 3D apparel visualization software in firefighting gear design and development.

## Results
The three key dimensions of the technological, organizational, and environmental framework were used to explore manufacturers’ perceptions of 3D apparel visualization software. The analysis revealed seven major categories, revealing how manufacturers perceive the value of the software for developing firefighting gear: relative advantage, technology maturity, and perceived future potential (Technological Context); organizational slack (Organizational Context); and industry characteristics, business partner readiness, and consumer perceptions (Environmental Context) (see Table [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#Tab2)). Due to the small, purposive sample (12 participants), the categories presented below are not generalizable to the broader population of firefighting gear manufacturers and should be viewed as context-specific and exploratory. The sections that follow present the categories associated with each context. Selected interview excerpts are included to support the findings, with participants identified by alphanumeric codes (e.g., A2, F1).
### Technological contexts: relative advantage, technology maturity, and perceived future potential
The technological context captures how participants evaluated the software’s immediate value and current limitations in relation to firefighting PPE design and decision-making. This section highlights how perceived benefits and concerns about capability shaped whether 3D apparel visualization software was viewed as a feasible support for development and communication.
#### Relative advantage
Relative advantage refers to the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea or practice it replaces (Rogers, 1983). In this study, relative advantage was reflected in three sub-categories that captured participants’ positive expectations and beliefs about the performance of 3D apparel visualization software: (1) assistive tool for pattern optimization and sample development, (2) visualizing air gap distribution, and (3) enhancing sales interactions with municipalities.
##### Assistive tool for pattern optimization and sample development
Both experienced and inexperienced users highlighted usefulness of 3D apparel visualization software for adjusting grading rules, fitting patterns on multi-size avatars to streamline manual grading, aligning pattern segments, interpreting draglines, and refining multilayered composites for structural and wildland firefighting gear. Several participants also emphasized the potential of dynamic avatars to assess firefighters’ range of motion and to evaluate compliance with NFPA fit requirements. Some participants also noted that virtual fitting could significantly reduce the time and cost associated with producing physical samples, particularly for high-value structural gear. Participant C1 explained that their company invests heavily in structural gear samples, noting that “These garments cost between $3,000 and $4,000, so considerable effort goes into ensuring everything is correct before the fabric is cut.” Participant B2 highlighted that the software enabled more precise evaluation of multilayer pattern configurations during turnout coat development: “We could assess the positioning of the armscye, sleeves, and segment endpoints to verify alignment and determine whether modifying the relative positioning of layers would improve the design.” Participant D1 further indicated that the software had streamlined the process by eliminating fitting stages that previously required hours or days to complete.
##### Visualizing air gap distribution
In addition to improving internal efficiency, the software provided notable benefits for external communication. Although some participants identified the need to accurately visualize air gaps in turnout gear as a challenge to adoption, others emphasized the value of 3D apparel visualization software in illustrating the volume and distribution of air gaps between the wearer and the structural ensemble, particularly through cross-sectional views and transparent material settings. Participant E1 noted that determining an optimal air gap that balances thermal protection and mobility remains an ongoing topic of discussion within the NFPA committee. Participant A1 stated that 3D apparel visualization software has the potential to facilitate consensus in this field as visual aids in technical discussions: “Some committee members assume that reducing a garment detail will make the entire uniform fit tightly. 3D virtual simulation image clarifies this misunderstanding by visualizing garment-to-body space and showing how to balance mobility with functional air gaps.”
##### Enhancing sales interactions with municipalities
Some participants proposed using images generated through 3D apparel visualization software to support discussions of product features when engaging with decision-makers from municipalities, fire departments, and county agencies. Participant B1 noted that virtual prototyping allows sales teams to present visual representations to customers without incurring the costs associated with fabric and labor. She explained:
> There is no clear visual representation of the gear until the department finalizes its purchase decision and specifications. With virtual prototyping, once those specifications are established, we can generate and present a 3D rendering of the product to show what the gear will look like.
#### Technology maturity
Despite the perceived advantages, participants reported that adoption is hindered by a lack of technology maturity. Technology maturity refers to the degree to which a technological architecture is sufficiently developed and ready for deployment within a firm (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                "), p. 193). In the present study, technology maturity was reflected in a sub-category that captured participants’ concerns about 3D apparel visualization software: simulation fidelity for composite materials, multilayer garments, and air gaps.
##### Simulation fidelity for composite materials, multilayer garments, and air gaps
A central concern among companies producing structural turnout gear was the limited capacity of current software to accurately simulate three-layer composite systems, seam construction, and the substantial ease and air gaps required for thermal protection. Structural turnout gear is composed of an outer shell, moisture barrier, and thermal liner, each contributing to overall performance (McQuerry et al., [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR41 "McQuerry, M., DenHartog, E., & Barker, R. \(2017\). Evaluating turnout composite layering strategies for reducing thermal burden in structural firefighter protective clothing systems. Textile Research Journal, 87\(10\), 1217–1225. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0040517516651101
                  
                ")). Participant C2 explained that the thickness of these layered materials influences seam construction, allowances, and sewing techniques, thereby increasing production complexity. Participant B2, who attempted to model the three layers by individually constructing and stacking pattern components, described the process as cumbersome: “We can assemble the patterns into a single product, but we can’t realistically observe how they hang on the body [in a simulation] because of the layering.”
The accurate representation of air gaps was also identified as a major challenge, as these spaces are critical for thermal protection. Participant C1 noted, “The wearer’s chest circumference is 44 inches, but the coat should measure 52 inches to maintain an adequate air buffer.” Participants emphasized that the degree of looseness required by safety standards makes it difficult to evaluate fit using 3D apparel visualization software. They also questioned the relevance of garment-fit maps, such as stress and strain indicators, which are more suited to body-conforming apparel than to protective garments designed with intentional and substantial ease.
In addition, participants pointed to the need for improved functionality to simulate the multiple garment layers worn in conjunction with turnout gear. Participant D1 expressed a desire for future systems to support stable multi-layer modeling of components such as fire hoods, suspenders, aluminized gear, and self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA). Collectively, these concerns highlight a gap between the current capabilities of 3D apparel visualization software and the specialized requirements of firefighting gear design.
#### Perceived future potential
Perceived future potential refers not to the current benefits that 3D apparel visualization software can provide, but to the hoped-for, future-oriented value that may emerge as the software evolves or is integrated with other systems and applications. This construct captures participants’ expectations for expanded capabilities or new functions they would like to see developed. In the present study, perceived future potential was reflected in two sub-categories: (1) predicting thermal protection and water penetration performance, and (2) enabling customization and enhancing customer engagement.
##### Predicting thermal protection and water penetration performance
Participants emphasized the need for the technology to transition from a primarily visual design tool to a performance-oriented evaluation platform. Participant C1 highlighted the potential of integrating predictive capabilities for thermal performance metrics, such as breathability and total heat loss. Participant C2 underscored this need, noting: “We allocate substantial resources to third-party testing for NFPA certification. It would be highly advantageous if the software could model how pattern modifications influence thermal and water penetration performance within a 3D simulation environment.”
Participant F1 further indicated that predictive modeling could enhance design decision-making, explaining that the ability to anticipate water penetration patterns during shower tests would support more effective pattern adjustments. He emphasized the potential to use such metrics to inform ventilation design and more sophisticated pattern engineering, while accounting for the interaction between garment construction and real-world fireground conditions.
##### Enabling customization and enhancing customer engagement
Participants further anticipated that future advancements could facilitate mass customization by enabling client-facing interfaces through which fire departments can directly specify gear configurations, reducing miscommunication and enhancing customer interaction. Two companies (E and F) were already providing customized firefighting gear. Participant E1 noted that current ordering processes rely on written or verbal exchanges with sales representatives, which can lead to misinterpretation. They expressed interest in developing “a 3D platform that allows customers to customize various elements of their gear and submit their designs for quotation.”
### Organizational contexts: organizational slack
Moving beyond perceptions of the technology itself, participants emphasized that adoption also depends on organizational conditions, particularly whether firms have sufficient discretionary resources to invest in licenses, infrastructure, and workforce upskilling. In this context, feasibility was discussed in terms of capacity to implement and sustain the workflow change.
#### Organizational slack
Participants reported that adoption decisions are heavily influenced by organizational slack. Organizational slack refers to the pool of uncommitted resources that a firm can draw upon to support new initiatives or innovations (Oliveira & Martins, [2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR51 "Oliveira, T., & Martins, M. F. \(2011\). Literature review of information technology adoption models at firm level. Electronic Journal of Information Systems Evaluation, 14\(1\), 110–121.")). In this study, organizational slack was reflected in two sub-categories: (1) financial costs, and (2) human capital investment.
##### Financial costs
Participants unanimously identified cost as the predominant barrier, with smaller firms struggling to justify the high price of software licenses and peripheral hardware (e.g., fabric testing devices, texture scanners) against the perceived return on investment, irrespective of their current usage status. Participant D1 articulated concerns, noting, “Our current system functions effectively, making it challenging to justify the annual software license fee, which previously quoted at $13,000.” For small- or medium-scale manufacturers operating within budget constraints, funds were prioritized for essential manufacturing production operations rather than for experimental 3D apparel visualization software over design or product innovation.
On the other hand, participants suggested that companies belonging to larger-scale firms would be willing to bear the cost if it was proven that using the software could improve the product quality. Participant F1 mentioned, “I believe that the software license fee is easily affordable for our company, and they are willing to invest heavily in proving that our product is the safest option available.” However, at the same time, he expected it would be challenging to communicate and prove the effectiveness of the software to the company decision-makers.
##### Human capital investment
Beyond financial costs, participants reported that the human capital investment required for implementation presented a significant hurdle. Both current users and non-users highlighted the steep learning curve and the substantial time required to upskill employees. Integrating the software into existing workflows demanded not just financial readiness but also a willingness to divert limited staff hours away from immediate production tasks to master complex new systems.
### Environmental contexts: industry characteristics, business partner readiness, and consumer perception
Finally, participants emphasized that adoption is influenced not only by internal readiness but also by environmental conditions, such as industry norms, partner capabilities, and end-user requirements. This section shows how ecosystem readiness and stakeholder acceptance can enable or constrain adoption even when the technology is promising and internal resources are available.
#### Industry characteristics
Within the TOE framework, industry characteristics refer to the structural and competitive attributes of the focal industry such as market structure, competitive intensity, regulatory policies, stakeholder pressures, industry life-cycle stage, and the availability of technology-related support that shape the external pressures and opportunities influencing innovation adoption (Gupta et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR79 "Gupta, S., Ghardallou, W., Pandey, D.                   https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ribaf.2022.101757
                  
                "); Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). In this study, industry characteristics were reflected in two sub-categories: (1) unchanging garment style requirements and (2) normative conservatism.
##### Unchanging garment style requirements
Unlike the fashion industry, where rapid style turnover drives the need for quick digital sampling, firefighting gear designs remain static for decades. Participant A2 noted, “Once we achieve a well-fitted shirt and pants, there's little need for alteration.” Similarly, Participant F1 observed, “Our repeat customers typically return every 5 or 10 years, requesting identical products.” and Participant C1 stated, “Our patterns have been 30 years old. Although we have newer patterns for new products, they possess similar properties.” Consequently, the infrequent need to adjust patterns or experiment with diverse virtual fabrics limits the utility of virtual fitting for pattern modifications or exploring fabric drapability and fit. The enduring nature of unaltered garment styles restricts the potential benefits and frequency of leveraging virtual fitting capabilities, as highlighted by participants.
##### Normative conservatism
Several participants perceived the firefighting gear industry as placing limited emphasis on design innovation and research. They noted that decision-makers tend to be conservative and less open to new technologies, partly due to the sector’s strong safety orientation. Participant E2 observed, “The scarcity of specialized designers for firefighter gear underscores its niche status.” Participant F1 mentioned, “A challenge in implementing this type of technology in this industry is overcoming the conservative mindset. Since safety is paramount, they tend to have a conservative mindset and are less receptive to new technology.” Participant E1 observed that the computer-aided design software used in the firefighting gear manufacturing industry lags compared to fields such as aerospace, automotive, and construction, reinforcing challenges to advancing innovative practices.
#### Business partner readiness
Adoption can be constrained by the lack of readiness across the broader value chain. Business partner readiness denotes the extent to which an organization’s external partners are prepared to integrate and support the use of new technologies (Malik et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR38 "Malik, S., Chadhar, M., Vatanasakdakul, S., & Chetty, M. \(2021\). Sustainability, 13\(16\), Article 9404. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/su13169404
                  
                ")). In the present study, business partner readiness was reflected in a single sub-category: preparedness among contract manufacturers.
##### Preparedness among contract manufacturers
Some participants expressed concern that manufacturers may lack the readiness to access and interpret data generated by 3D apparel visualization software. Participants A1 and A2 noted that their company’s reliance on contract manufacturers could limit their ability to adopt such technology. Participant A2 explained that the software is still unfamiliar within the firefighting gear industry, which may lead to implementation challenges and potential issues. They stated:
> We are required to work with certain manufacturers under NFPA 1977 standards, and the ones we have collaborated with so far have not used this type of technology. It is unclear how adoption will translate if we continue working with them. Training will be necessary, but more importantly, these manufacturers must be willing to embrace the change
#### Consumer perception
Consumer perception refers to end users’ attitudes toward innovative technologies (Schmitt et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-026-00469-8#ref-CR85 "Schmitt, G., Mladenow, A., Strauss, C., & Schaff hauser-Linzatti, M. \(2019\). Smart contracts and internet of things: A qualitative content analysis using the technology-organization-environment framework to identify key-determinants. Procedia Computer Science, 160, 189–196. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procs.2019.09.460
                  
                ")). Consumer perception was reflected in a single sub-category, with participants expressing skepticism toward virtual simulation in customer-facing marketing, based on their understanding of consumer perceptions.
##### Skepticism toward virtual simulation in customer-facing marketing
None of the participants viewed virtual simulation images as effective for marketing on websites, social media, or product catalogs. They expressed that the industry culture heavily favors “real-world” testimonials and physical handling of gear over digital simulations, limiting the technology’s effectiveness as a marketing tool unless it can be proven to accurately reflect physical performance. Participant A2 explained that using simulated or artificial models would not effectively support building a genuine connection with the firefighter community. Similarly, Participant E4 noted that their company deliberately features real firefighters, emphasizing that their lived experience with the gear enhances credibility among other firefighters.
Several categories and subcategories within the TOE framework were identified as shaping firefighting gear manufacturers’ perceptions of 3D apparel visualization software. Within the technological context, participants viewed 3D apparel visualization software as a performance-oriented tool suited to the unique demands of protective gear. They highlighted its potential to optimize patterns for bulky, multilayered ensembles, visualize air gap distribution, and use dynamic avatars to assess mobility and NFPA fit compliance. Participants also noted value in using 3D apparel visualization software as a sales aid when meeting with municipal buyers and fire departments. Looking ahead, they anticipated future capabilities such as predicting thermal and water-penetration performance and enabling more interactive customization.
In terms of the organizational context, adoption was limited by financial constraints, the need to upskill staff, and the challenge of integrating 3D apparel visualization software into existing workflows that would support digital assets and simulation processes. For the environmental context, the industry’s niche, safety-driven culture, stable garment designs, and reliance on contract manufacturers unfamiliar with 3D virtual simulation data reduced perceived necessity and feasibility. Participants also noted that firefighters and municipal buyers prefer marketing featuring real firefighters, making simulated imagery less persuasive. ## Discussion
Overall, this study provides insight into how firefighting PPE manufacturers evaluate the adoption of 3D apparel visualization software and the conditions that shape its feasibility in a safety–critical, standards-constrained product domain. Consistent with TOE-based technology adoption research, participants’ evaluations reflected an interaction among technological characteristics, organizational readiness, and environmental constraints. However, rather than viewing 3D apparel visualization software as a direct substitute for physical sampling and testing, participants framed it as a complementary capability whose perceived relative advantage and compatibility depend on two domain-specific requirements: effective integration with established verification routines (e.g., wear trials, iterative sampling, and performance compliance testing) and acceptance by key external stakeholders as a credible visual aid for supporting regulatory communication, sales interactions, and customer engagement. Taken together, these findings suggest that in regulated, high-risk manufacturing contexts, TOE constructs such as relative advantage and compatibility are shaped by legitimacy and validation expectations that extend beyond the firm to the broader industry community and supply chain.
Consistent with classic TOE theorizing, manufacturers emphasized relative advantage as a necessary but insufficient condition for adoption (Rogers, 1983; Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990). Participants recognized clear performance benefits of 3D apparel visualization software, including pattern optimization, air gap visualization, and improved communication with municipal buyers. The categories of perceived future potential further clarify how firms navigate emerging technologies. Participants articulated a strong vision for 3D apparel visualization software as a functional evaluation platform capable of predicting thermal protection and water resistance or supporting client-facing customization. This suggests that the technology occupies a pre-strategic phase in which manufacturers recognize its long-term potential but remain constrained by doubts about short-term functional validity and return on investment. Theoretically, this underscores the importance of distinguishing between present relative advantage and future-oriented value within the technological dimension of the TOE framework, particularly for emerging simulation tools in safety–critical contexts.
Within the organizational context, the findings underscore organizational slack as a pivotal factor shaping adoption. Company size further impacted how the TOE dimensions interacted. Participants consistently noted that the high costs associated with software licenses, testing equipment, and staff training were particularly prohibitive for small and medium-sized manufacturers operating under tight margins. Nevertheless, larger firms were more likely to have adopted or to be piloting 3D apparel visualization software, reflecting their greater organizational slack and access to training resources. For instance, larger firms were either already using 3D apparel visualization software (Companies B and D) or actively exploring its implementation (Company F). A participant from Company F noted that designers had begun receiving software training and described adoption decisions as being driven by demonstrable utility rather than cost considerations.
Notably, even among firms already experimenting with 3D apparel visualization software, adoption was not characterized as a discrete purchasing decision but rather as a multi-year organizational commitment involving skill development, workflow reconfiguration, and the curation of digital assets. By contrast, smaller firms (Companies A, C, and E) engaged in more risk-averse evaluations. Limited technological exposure and reliance on established production processes constrained organizational slack and heightened perceived implementation risk, leading anticipated operational disruptions to outweigh potential benefits.

Company type (firefighting gear type) further influenced these evaluations through the organizational dimension. For wildland gear manufacturers (Companies A and D), whose products involve relatively simple, single-layer constructions, the technology presented low perceived complexity and minimal strategic relevance. Structural turnout gear manufacturers (Companies B, C, E, and F), however, confronted high technological complexity due to multilayer composites and intricate construction requirements. This complexity simultaneously intensified skepticism about current simulation fidelity and elevated expectations for future capabilities such as garment fit maps and performance prediction including inter-garment tension, total heat loss, water penetration, and wearer fit satisfaction during pattern design. Structural turnout gear production heightened both perceived technological barriers and perceived technological advantage, reflecting the ambivalent role of innovation complexity in shaping adoption intentions.
The study findings also underscore the importance of the environmental context in ways that extend typical TOE applications. Firefighting gear manufacturers operate within stringent standards, liability concerns, and procurement norms, all which frame how new technologies are interpreted and assessed. These environmental pressures intersected with product characteristics to shape firms’ evaluations of technological attributes. Manufacturers of wildland gear viewed 3D apparel visualization software as offering mainly incremental improvements in pattern making. In contrast, structural turnout gear manufacturers faced far higher technical demands due to multilayer composites and strict thermal performance requirements. For them, 3D apparel visualization software elicited both heightened expectations and deeper skepticism: it was imagined as a promising tool for fit mapping and performance prediction, yet current software was widely regarded as technically inadequate. This ambivalence suggests that greater product complexity can simultaneously amplify perceived relative advantage and perceived complexity, creating an innovation tension within the technological domain.

Technological limitations such as imperfect simulation of composite materials, air gap layers, and multilayer garments shaped organizational risk assessments, which were then evaluated within environmental regimes that demand strong evidence of safety and compliance. Even when 3D apparel visualization software is viewed as beneficial for design efficiency, manufacturers remain cautious if the tools do not clearly support, or are not formally recognized within, established performance evaluation and certification processes. The findings demonstrate that, in functional and safety–critical apparel contexts, adoption is shaped by the interaction of multiple TOE-based mechanisms. These include technological factors such as simulation fidelity for multilayer systems and air gap representation; organizational factors such as internal expertise, resource availability, and workflow integration; and environmental factors such as regulatory expectations, partner readiness, and user trust. Within this configuration, the perceived credibility and legitimacy of digital outputs play a central role. As a result, technical capability alone is insufficient to explain adoption. The same 3D tool may be interpreted as a low-risk communication aid or as a high-stakes performance-evidence system depending on product complexity, organizational capacity, and perceived regulatory scrutiny.
More broadly, the study highlights the relevance of these mechanisms beyond the immediate context of firefighting gear. In functional and protective apparel sectors where product performance is closely tied to regulation, specialized materials, and interorganizational coordination, adoption is likely to be shaped by similar interactions among technological readiness, organizational capacity, and environmental legitimacy. In this sense, the contribution of the study lies not in statistical generalizability but in the transferability of these TOE-based mechanisms to other safety–critical apparel domains, such as military, industrial, or medical protective clothing. At the same time, these implications should be interpreted within the scope of the present study. The findings are based on qualitative interviews with a small sample of U.S. firefighting gear manufacturers and therefore reflect a context-specific analysis. The insights are most applicable to sectors that share comparable technological constraints, regulatory environments, and supply-chain structures, rather than to the apparel industry as a whole.
The findings also highlight practical implications for software developers, manufacturers, and regulatory bodies. For developers, closer collaboration with protective clothing manufacturers and textile laboratories is needed to improve the physics-based modeling of multilayer composites and to strengthen validation by linking simulations to established metrics such as Thermal Protective Performance (TPP) and Total Heat Loss (THL). These improvements could support the transition of 3D apparel visualization software from a communication-focused tool to a platform capable of informing performance-oriented design decisions. In addition, vendors may reduce adoption barriers by offering modular features, streamlined onboarding processes, and technical support that extends to contract manufacturers and other supply-chain partners.
For regulatory bodies such as NFPA and related standards committees, the findings underscore the importance of considering how virtual prototyping tools may complement, rather than replace, existing certification processes. Providing clear guidance on the appropriate use of 3D apparel visualization software in early-stage design, for example as a screening tool prior to physical TPP and THL testing, could reduce perceived risk and encourage adoption. Over time, the development of standardized practices for documenting digital simulations, input parameters, and validation procedures may enable more systematic integration of virtual evidence into design and certification workflows.
Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study focuses on firm-level perspectives and relies on a single data source, interviews with a relatively small sample, which constrains the scope of the findings. The limited diversity of the sample suggests that the results should be interpreted as exploratory and context-specific rather than representative of the broader industry. In addition, the concentration of participant roles, primarily designers and technical specialists, limits insight into perspectives from production, marketing, and executive decision-making. Some participants also had limited direct experience with 3D apparel visualization software, which may have influenced their perceptions.
Future research should continue to evaluate the validity and accuracy of virtual simulation technologies while expanding beyond a single national context. Advancing 3D apparel visualization software for firefighting gear will require improvements in simulation precision, enhanced garment fit mapping, and more standardized methods for digitizing multilayer composite materials and trims. Identifying the optimal air gap that balances thermal protection and mobility remains a critical step for informing fit standards and improving simulation accuracy. At the same time, fit map functionality should be further developed to better predict garment tension and stress distribution while incorporating air gap parameters.
To strengthen the robustness and broader applicability of this research, future studies could adopt mixed-methods designs that integrate qualitative insights with quantitative and experimental approaches. Comparing virtual simulation outputs with laboratory-based measurements of thermal performance, air gap distribution, and mobility would provide important validation benchmarks. Larger-scale surveys and comparative studies across different regions, including Europe and Asia, would further clarify how regulatory environments and industry structures shape technology adoption. These efforts would contribute to the development of more reliable simulation tools and more widely applicable guidelines for virtual prototyping in protective clothing design.
